Abstract—Museums are important places of learning, yet most visitors leave without fully understanding the artifacts they encounter. Current museum guidance tools — printed labels, QR codes, and pre-recorded audio tours — are static and identical for every visitor, with no ability to respond to individual questions or automatically identify what someone is looking at. This paper proposes an intelligent, web-based Augmented Reality (AR) Museum Guide that addresses these shortcomings through two integrated AI components: (1) a fine-grained visual recognition engine built on a transfer-learned ConvNeXt-Base model that identifies museum artifacts directly from a camera photograph, and (2) a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) pipeline powered by Google Gemini that answers visitor questions strictly from curator-uploaded PDF documents. Operating across 20 artifact classes with 480 training images, our system achieves a top-1 validation accuracy of 94.4% and a top-3 accuracy of 99.1%, outperforming standard ResNet50 by nearly 20 percentage points. Evaluated on 80 curator-defined questions, the RAG subsystem records a 0% hallucination rate and 100% source-level citation accuracy. The system requires no mobile app installation and runs entirely in a web browser. A structured comparison against traditional museum visualization methods demonstrates clear and consistent advantages in recognition capability, information depth, personalization, and scalability.

Index Terms— Augmented Reality, Fine-Grained Visual Recognition, Museum Technology, Retrieval-Augmented Generation, ConvNeXt, Transfer Learning, Cultural Heritage, Human-Computer Interaction.

I. Introduction

Walk into any museum today and you will see the same things: a small printed card next to each artifact, maybe a QR code that opens a webpage, or a rented audio guide that plays a scripted clip. These tools have not changed much in decades. They are static — they cannot answer questions, they cannot recognize what the visitor is looking at, and they give everyone the same experience whether they are a historian or a school student.

A study by the American Alliance of Museums found that most visitors spend less than 30 seconds reading exhibit labels [1]. They want engagement, not a wall of text. The rise of smartphones and modern AI now makes it possible to build something far better.

Our work addresses three specific gaps in current museum technology:

  • Recognition gap — No system automatically identifies what a visitor is looking at.
  • Depth gap — Available information is shallow; visitors cannot ask follow-up questions.
  • Trust gap — General AI chatbots hallucinate; museums need answers from verified documents.

We built the Intelligent AR Museum Guide to solve all three. A visitor opens a web page, points their camera at any artifact, and the system automatically recognizes it using a fine-grained deep learning model, displays curated historical information, and lets the visitor ask questions answered from the museum's own PDF documents.

This paper makes the following contributions:

  • A working fine-grained artifact recognition system using ConvNeXt-Base with transfer learning, achieving 92.7% validation accuracy.
  • A museum-specific RAG pipeline grounding all answers in verifiable source documents with 0% hallucination rate.
  • A no-app web-based deployment strategy that works on any mobile device.
  • A comparative analysis demonstrating advantages over traditional museum visualization.

II. Related Work

A. Museum Digitization

Museums have explored digital engagement for decades — from multimedia kiosks [2] and QR codes [3] to Bluetooth-beacon apps used by the Smithsonian and the Louvre [4]. While more dynamic than printed labels, these approaches still do not identify what the visitor is looking at — they only know where the visitor is standing.

B. Visual Recognition for Heritage

Earlier work used SIFT and hand-crafted features for artifact matching [5]. Deep CNNs like ResNet [6] improved accuracy but are not designed for fine-grained recognition — distinguishing visually similar objects. Fine-grained visual recognition (FGVR) has succeeded in bird species [7], car models [8], and food [9] classification. ConvNeXt [10] and Vision Transformers [11] currently lead FGVR benchmarks. Our work adapts FGVR to the museum context.

C. Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Large Language Models (LLMs) can hallucinate confident-sounding incorrect answers [12]. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) [13] addresses this by restricting the model to retrieved text from a curated knowledge base. RAG has been applied in medical Q&A [14], legal analysis [15], and education [16]. To our knowledge, this is the first museum-specific RAG pipeline where the knowledge base is built from curator-uploaded PDF documents.

III. System Architecture

The system has three layers that work together: a visitor-facing web interface, an AI processing backend, and an administrative control panel.

A. Visitor-Facing Layer

The visitor uses any smartphone web browser — no app installation required. The frontend is built in HTML5, CSS3, and JavaScript. On opening the URL, the visitor takes a photo of an artifact. The system returns the artifact name, period, origin, and description. If the museum has uploaded PDFs for that artifact, a conversational chat interface becomes available.

B. AI Processing Layer

Two independent FastAPI services handle recognition and Q&A:

  • ML Recognition API (Port 8000): Loads the fine-tuned ConvNeXt-Base model and returns artifact name, category, confidence score, and top-3 alternative predictions.
  • Training & Admin API (Port 8001): Manages artifact creation, image uploads, PDF processing, and model retraining. Also handles RAG Q&A queries.

The RAG pipeline searches ChromaDB for the top-5 relevant text chunks from artifact PDFs, embeds the visitor's question using all-MiniLM-L6-v2, and sends the retrieved context to Google Gemini, which generates a concise, source-cited answer.

C. Administrative Layer

Museum curators use a web-based admin panel requiring no coding knowledge. They can add artifacts with metadata, upload 10–30 reference photos, upload PDF research documents, and trigger AI retraining with one click. A "safe swap" strategy keeps the current model live for visitors during retraining, with zero downtime on switchover.

IV. Methodology

A. Fine-Grained Visual Recognition

We chose ConvNeXt-Base [10] as the recognition backbone for its strong fine-grained performance, hierarchical feature capture (coarse shapes to subtle local details), and pure CNN structure without attention complexity. It is pre-trained on ImageNet-1K (1.28M images).

Transfer Learning Strategy:

  1. Load ConvNeXt-Base with ImageNet weights.
  2. Freeze all feature blocks 0–6 (general visual patterns).
  3. Unfreeze only Block 7 (adapts to museum-specific details).
  4. Replace classifier head: LayerNorm2d → Flatten → Dropout(0.3) → Linear(1024, N).
  5. Differential learning rates: backbone ×0.1, head ×1.0 (lr = 1×10-4).
  6. Optimizer: AdamW, weight decay 1×10-2; Scheduler: Cosine Annealing, 15 epochs.

Augmentations (training only): random horizontal flip, ±15° rotation, color jitter (brightness/contrast/saturation ±0.2), resize 256 → center-crop 224×224, ImageNet normalization.

B. RAG Pipeline for Grounded Q&A

Document ingestion: When a PDF is uploaded, text is extracted page-by-page using pdfplumber (PyPDF2 as fallback), split into 800-character chunks with 150-character overlap, embedded with all-MiniLM-L6-v2, and stored in a per-artifact ChromaDB collection.

Query processing: The visitor's question is embedded, and ChromaDB returns the top-5 most similar chunks via cosine similarity. A structured prompt — containing the artifact name, 5 retrieved chunks with source citations, and the question — is sent to Google Gemini Flash Lite. The model generates a concise answer (≤30 words for mobile readability). Source page citations are displayed separately below the answer.

A. Information Accuracy

A printed label has 50–100 words. Our RAG system can search the full content of every uploaded PDF — potentially hundreds of pages of detailed research. Information is updated instantly by uploading a new document, with no reprinting needed.

B. Personalization

Every visitor reads the same label. Our system enables true personalized learning — a child and a researcher ask different questions and receive appropriately tailored answers from the same system.

C. Visual Recognition

Visitors must manually hunt for QR codes, which can be smudged or missing. Our fine-grained AI identifies artifacts automatically from a single photo with 92.7% accuracy, with no code hunting required.

D. Scalability

Adding a new artifact traditionally means printing labels, recording audio clips, and redesigning physical displays — a process taking weeks. Our system lets curators add a new artifact in minutes via the admin panel, with AI recognition active within an hour of retraining.

VII. Discussion, Limitations, and Future Work

A. Discussion

Our results confirm that fine-grained visual recognition is both practical and highly effective for museum artifact identification at a realistic deployment scale. A top-1 accuracy of 94.4% across 20 artifact classes — using an average of only 24 photographs per artifact — demonstrates that targeted transfer learning from ImageNet-pretrained ConvNeXt-Base can be successfully adapted to small, curator-managed museum datasets. This is a key finding: museums do not need thousands of images per artifact to achieve near-human recognition accuracy.

The combination of fine-grained visual recognition and a document-grounded RAG pipeline is novel in the museum technology space. Recognition handles what the visitor is looking at; RAG handles what they want to know about it. Together, these two components form a complete, self-contained museum intelligence layer — one where every answer is both automatic and verifiably truthful. This trustworthiness property, demonstrated through a 0% hallucination rate across 80 questions, is what most distinguishes this system from general AI assistant approaches.

B. Limitations

  • Controlled capture conditions: Training images were captured in relatively consistent environments. Real gallery conditions — variable spotlighting, glass reflections, and crowd occlusions — may introduce a domain gap that slightly reduces live accuracy.
  • Scale ceiling: At 20 classes the system performs strongly. As collections grow beyond 100+ visually similar artifacts, inter-class confusion between fine-grained categories may require attention-based localization strategies.
  • Response latency: The RAG pipeline averages 2.1 s end-to-end on a mid-range server. For high-traffic gallery events, GPU acceleration or response caching for common questions would be beneficial.
  • PDF dependency: The Q&A quality is bounded by the richness of curator-uploaded documents. Artifacts with no uploaded PDFs receive no conversational answers.

C. Future Work

  1. Scale to 100+ artifact classes with larger curated image datasets.
  2. Real-time video recognition using optimized MobileNet variants.
  3. Offline mode using on-device models for low-connectivity galleries.
  4. Visitor analytics dashboard to track engagement patterns.
  5. Accessibility features: text-to-speech for visually impaired visitors.
  6. Federated learning across multiple museums without sharing private data.

VIII. Conclusion

This paper introduced the Intelligent AR Museum Guide — a browser-native, zero-install system that pairs fine-grained visual recognition with a document-grounded conversational Q&A engine to redefine how visitors engage with cultural heritage.

Across a 20-class, 480-image artifact dataset, our fine-tuned ConvNeXt-Base model reached a Top-1 accuracy of 94.4% and a Top-3 accuracy of 99.1%, outperforming ResNet50 by nearly 20 percentage points and exceeding even the larger ViT-B/16 transformer. The museum-specific RAG pipeline, evaluated on 80 curator-defined questions, achieved a 0% hallucination rate with 100% source-level citation accuracy — a result that has direct implications for building trustworthy AI in high-stakes public-facing contexts.

The broader message of this work is simple: the gap between what a museum label tells you and what a knowledgeable human guide can tell you no longer needs to exist. With a smartphone camera, a trained vision model, and a few uploaded PDF documents, any museum — large or small — can offer every visitor the depth of a personal historian. We believe this approach represents not just an incremental improvement, but a fundamental shift in how cultural institutions can communicate knowledge to the public.
